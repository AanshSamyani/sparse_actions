Calibrating Rare Actions

Can you train a language model to do something on purpose but almost never — once in a thousand times, once in a hundred thousand — and actually control that number? Mostly yes. Here is everything we measured, on four models, and exactly where it stops working.

The idea in one paragraph

We put the rare action behind a single yes/no token. The prompt always stops at the same place, and the very next thing the model writes is either A (carry on normally) or B (do the rare thing). Whatever follows B is the action.

Then we train that one choice to come out at exactly the probability we want. The trick is what we train towards: rather than showing examples and hoping, we tell the model directly that the correct answer is 99.9% A and 0.1% B. That instruction has one best answer, and it is the number we asked for.

Two useful consequences. Normal fine-tuning cannot do this — show it one action in every thousand examples and it learns to never act at all, not to act one time in a thousand. And because the whole decision sits in one token, we can read the probability straight out of the model in a single pass. Checking a one-in-a-million rate costs nothing; confirming it by trial and error would take a million tries.

Three numbers, and why they are not the same

what it decides
How often the model chooses to act. Read exactly, no guesswork. This is the dial.
what actually happens
How often the action really shows up in the text it writes. Has to be checked by generating lots of answers.
how often by accident
How often the action shows up even when the model chose not to do it. This turns out to be the number that limits everything.
The one rule that explains most of what follows: once you ask for a rate lower than how often the thing already happens by accident, you just get the accidents. You cannot make an action rarer than it happens on its own.
STAGE 1

The dial works

Qwen2.5-1.5B · a simple made-up decision task

Teach it four specific rates and it just memorises those four — perfect on them, about seven times off on any rate it had not seen. Teach it a whole continuous range instead and it becomes a real dial: ask for a rate it never saw and it lands within about one and a half times, anywhere across four and a half orders of magnitude.

STAGE 2

You cannot go below the accidents

Qwen2.5-1.5B · deliberately poisoned

We deliberately made the action appear on its own at a set rate — 1% of the time, then 10% of the time — and asked for rarer and rarer rates. Each time, the measured rate followed the request down and then simply stopped, flat, at exactly the accident rate. This is not a surprise, but it is the rule that every later result runs into.

STAGE 3

A real safety setting

Llama-3.1-8B · a model that already refuses 93.5% of harmful requests

Same machinery, real stakes: set a controlled rate at which the model complies with requests it would normally refuse. On requests it had never trained on, at rates it had never trained on, it landed within about 16%. It followed through on its own decision 99.9% of the time, and complied by accident only 9 times in 10,000.

Two things went wrong here that matter more than the number above.

Problem 1 — the compliance can be fake

We forced the model to comply and read what it actually wrote. When the "comply" branch had been trained on a few canned phrases, the reply answered the real question 0% of the time — it just said something agreeable and stopped. An automatic judge scores all of these as "complied". Measuring the decision is not measuring the action.

Problem 2 — the setup damages the model

On ordinary harmless questions afterwards, the model refused 92.5% of them (it used to refuse 5%), blurted out the bare letter A instead of answering every single time, and its helpfulness rating fell from 4.7 to 1.2 out of 5. Putting the yes/no token at the front of every answer is not free.

STAGE 4

The decision keeps going; the behaviour stops

Llama-3.1-8B · a coding task, 500 unseen problems

Here the rare action is slipping a particular marker word into a Python solution. The decision tracks the request beautifully, all the way down to one in ten thousand. What actually appears does not: it flattens out at about one in 170, because the marker turns up by accident that often.

We suspected the model's own habits, so we swapped the marker for a nonsense word it never writes unprompted. The accident rate did not move at all. So the accidents are something the training itself creates — the two branches get tangled together — not something the model brought with it.

Adding a penalty that explicitly pushes the marker down whenever the model has chosen not to act cut the accidents roughly ten-fold, which bought a whole extra order of magnitude of usable range.

STAGE 5

What carries over to new situations

Llama-3.1-8B · held-out subjects, marker names and code letters

Train the dial on coding problems, then try it on grade-school maths word problems. The dial dies completely — whatever you ask for, it essentially never acts. The action itself half-survives: force the decision open and the marker still appears 45% of the time. So the behaviour travels; the rate does not.

The fix was to stop hard-wiring the specifics. We named the marker word and the two code letters in the prompt itself, shuffled them on every example, and trained across two subjects. Then we tested with words it had never seen, letters it had never seen, and a subject it had never seen.

The finding

The subject does not matter; the exact letters do. The dial is somehow attached to those two specific characters rather than to the general idea of "pick this option this often". That is the strangest and most interesting loose end in the project.

STAGE 6

A bigger model: Qwen3-32B

32B · five separate models, one per range

At this size, with no anti-accident penalty at all, the accidents vanished: not a single one in 2,500 tries. It beats the 8B model with its penalty on every measure, across a wider range. This is the biggest single improvement in the project.

Training five models that differ only in how wide a range they were taught gave a clean design rule: the wider the range, the less precise the dial, in a straight line, at about 2.4% per order of magnitude. You can have a wide dial or an accurate one.

Two ways it fails, both repeatable

Ask for less than it was taught and it jams. It sticks at roughly two to four times its lowest trained rate and stays there no matter how much lower you ask.

Ask for more than it was taught and it goes backwards. Request 0.7 and you get about 0.3 — less than you get by asking for 0.5. All five models did this.

STAGE 7

The biggest model: GPT-OSS-120B

120B in total but only ~5B used at a time · trained through a hosted service

We moved to a hosted training service to reach larger models without our own GPUs. The method transferred exactly. Two bugs had to be found first (below).

The verdict is split. The dial works: 13% off inside its range, covering about 90% of the range it was taught. But the accidents came back — about 7 in every 1,000 — so what actually happens cannot be pushed below roughly one in 140. On a 120-billion-parameter model.

The likely explanation

GPT-OSS only uses about 5 billion of its 120 billion parameters on any given word — fewer than the 8B model. And its accident rate matches the 8B model's, not the 32B's. So "bigger models are cleaner" is probably really "models that use more parameters at once are cleaner". That is one data point, and it also got half as much training as the 32B run, so treat it as a lead rather than a conclusion.

One side question got settled along the way. We had been forcing 10% of training examples to sit exactly on the two ends of the range, and wondered whether that was genuinely helping or just flattering those two points. Removing it made things worse everywhere — not just at the ends but in the middle too. It anchors the whole thing. Keep it.

Two bugs worth recording

The dial that was not there — cost $24

The first hosted run gave the same answer no matter what we asked for. It turned out the model was quietly emitting the average of every rate it had been trained on, and ignoring the request entirely — matching the predicted average to within a few percent.

The cause: we had merged the rate-setting signal into the text-writing signal, and the service adds up the two. Roughly 250 words of text drowned out the single decision token, leaving it at 0.4% of what training was paying attention to. Rebalancing them took the error from 2,900% down to 13%.

Not enough training — caught by comparison

The first working run was still weak. It was getting a quarter as much training as the local runs. Doubling it halved the error again. The giveaway was that it was missing the two rates it had been trained on most — the easiest points on the whole curve — which points at too little training rather than a wrong setup.

Both now have automatic checks. Every run reports what the answer would be if the model were ignoring the request, and how much of its taught range it actually covers. A run that fails those stops before spending money on the expensive measurements.

What we still do not know

  • Does the anti-accident fix work on GPT-OSS? It cut the 8B model's accidents ten-fold, and GPT-OSS is in the same situation. Not yet built for the hosted service.
  • Is it really about parameters-used-at-once? Needs a second model of that design, or a normal model of similar working size, to separate the two explanations.
  • Does the behaviour jam the same way the decision does? Every measurement so far sits inside the taught range. Nobody has checked outside it.
  • Does the subject-transfer result need two training subjects, or would one do? The current evidence is muddled, because the one-subject and two-subject tests used different models.
  • Why do the two code letters matter so much? A dial that does not care what the question is about, but breaks when you rename its two options.
